statistics (Lecture1)
*Mathematical statistics: development, derivation and proofs of
statistical theorems, formulas and rules
*Applied statistics:
*Descriptive statistics /singular/ – methods for organizing
and summarizing information (with the use of tables,
graphs and simple measures); the aim is to describe
properties of a data set; no generalization!
*Inferential statistics /sg./ – the aim is to
draw general conclusions about the
population based on information obtained
from a random sample (data set)

=== SLIDE 4 ===
Population vs. sample, I.
*Population: a group of statistical elements
*population size (N) is often very large (and thus it is
practically impossible to study the whole population)
*we examine population’s properties through a
sample
*Sample: a data set (subset of a population) that
originated on the basis of some sampling
statistical survey (procedure)
*Using the knowledge gained from the sample, we
can draw inferences concerning the population
and its properties.

=== SLIDE 6 ===
Population vs. sample:
types of statistical
surveys
*population survey (census)
*is performed on all N elements of the population
*characteristics (parameters) of the population
can be calculated; descriptive statistics
*sample survey
*is performed only on n elements selected from
the population
*characteristics (parameters) of the population
can only be estimated

=== SLIDE 7 ===
Population vs. sample,
III.
*Why not just examine the population
directly?
*Some populations are too large.
*Data from the sample survey are easier
(and cheaper) to process.
*The sample survey may be more detailed.
*Some population surveys are impossible to be
processed (e.g., automobile crash-test).
*Sometimes it is necessary to have the results
before the population survey (e.g., public
opinion polls before elections).

=== SLIDE 10 ===
Two statistical “worlds”,
I.
*First, an “empirical world”, i.e.
*the world of given (known) data sets
*the world of calculated descriptive statistics
(value of arithmetic mean, value of sample
standard deviation etc.)
*all symbols in the list of formulas (pgs. 1, 2, 3)
represent numbers (calculated values)
*inferential statistics: uncertainty about
theoretical assumptions
10
=== SLIDE 11 ===
Two statistical “worlds”,
II.
*Second, a “theoretical world”, i.e.
*the world of random variables
*the world of probability, random events and
random variables’ probability distributions
*some symbols in the formulas (list of formulas:
pgs. 7, 8; „T“ on page 6) represent random
variables
*inferential statistics: satisfied
theoretical assumptions
11
=== SLIDE 12 ===
Two statistical “worlds”,
III.
*inferential statistics
*in “theoretical world”: formulas are derived
under theoretical scenarios with perfectly
satisfied theoretical assumptions
*in “empirical world”: used in real-life
scenarios with uncertainty, if theoretical
assumptions are satisfied (and how much) or
not; all conclusions based on calculations with
a data in random sample contain uncertainty
12
=== SLIDE 13 ===
Two statistical “worlds”,
IV.
*descriptive statistics /singular/: practical
analysis completely carried out in the
empirical“world”
*inferential statistics: practical analysis
completely carried out in the empirical
“world” using statistical tools/methods
derived in the theoretical“world”

=== SLIDE 24 ===
Sampling error
*the specific value of a sample statistic
(calculated from one sample) will differ
from a parameter (characteristic) that
is being estimated
*don’t forget that the results are only
estimates of some property (parameter,
distribution, …) of the population…
*inferential statistics: there is always
uncertainty in results/conclusions
24
=== SLIDE 25 ===
Interval estimates
*interval estimate– the interval is
constructed around a point estimate
*it’s likely to cover the (unknown value of)
population parameter
*contains more information than the point
estimate
*each interval is constructed with regard
to a given confidence level 1-a and is
called a 100(1-a)% confidence interval
*e.g., 1-a = 0.95: 95% confidence interval

=== SLIDE 46 ===
46
Usefulness of
constructed (empirical)
confidence intervals
*may be used to judge a research hypothesis
*The research hypothesis must be formulated
before any calculations are carried out (before
drawing the random sample)!
*e.g.: proportion of expired cans in all
warehouses is 15 %
*constructed interval (0.117, 0.251) supports the
research hypothesis as it covers 0.15
*the higher the number of constructed intervals,
the stronger the support of the research hyp.
